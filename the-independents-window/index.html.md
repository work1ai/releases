*01 · The gap*

## What the large operators already automate, *and how independent and mid-sized firms close the gap.*

The large operators (national managers, institutional owner-operators and the biggest regional firms) bought scale: central teams, standard systems and round-the-clock coverage. They hold a growing share of the market:

- **1 in 5** purpose-built units in Calgary and Edmonton belongs to a handful of owner-operators. (Firm filings)
- **1 in 7** BC strata lots: the equivalent of what the three largest strata managers that publish counts claim to manage. (Firm-stated)
- **9+** BC and Alberta management businesses, divisions or contract books bought by national and regional managers since 2023.

Each row below is a piece of that scale an independent or mid-sized firm can now reach with AI, as long as a person keeps the decisions that matter. We give no time-savings estimates: nothing is promised until it has been measured in the firm itself.

| Work | How large operators run it | Common pattern below national scale | With AI, governed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leasing enquiries | Central leasing teams, CRM and virtual leasing agents. One large owner-operator reports tour-to-lease conversion up 33% and replies in minutes, not days. (Firm-stated) | Shared inbox and phone, answered in office hours by the same people who handle maintenance. | Every enquiry answered and qualified within minutes, showings booked to a calendar; a person approves the applicant. |
| Maintenance requests | Portals, triage rules, vendor networks; one large commercial landlord reports 4–6% energy savings and 10–15% fewer work orders from building AI. (Firm-stated) | Calls and texts to the manager’s phone; trade choice from memory. | Requests logged, sorted by urgency and drafted as work orders with the right trade; a person approves spend over a set limit. |
| Rent and arrears | Pre-authorised debit, portals and automatic reminders. | A mix of debit, e-Transfer and cheques, matched by hand. | Payments matched to tenants, reminders sent on schedule; a person decides on every notice. |

The middle column describes patterns that are common across the industry. It is not a description of any one firm.

*The opportunity*

### Scale used to be the only way to run a portfolio efficiently. *AI changes what scale was buying.*

National and institutional operators pay for central leasing teams, 24-hour response, standard owner reporting and compliance staff. Much of that work is reading, sorting, drafting and checking, which is exactly what AI now does well. A firm below national scale that has written down how it works can buy that capability without the overhead, and keep the personal service that wins owners in the first place.

- **The constraint: Manager time.** Units per manager, not demand, limits growth for most independent firms.
- **The lever: Written-down work.** A process someone else could follow is a process software can help with.
- **The safeguard: Decide first.** Name the decisions a person must keep before anything is automated.

*02 · State of technology*

## Mostly one ledger, *and AI that has barely started.*

By its vendor’s account, Canadian property management runs largely on one family of accounting software, with marketing, screening and resident portals bolted on around it. AI use is growing quickly from a low base, and most of it is chat and analytics, not automated work.

#### Businesses that used AI in the past 12 months

Real estate and rental and leasing, second quarter of each year. Letters are Statistics Canada’s quality grades. (Statistics Canada)

BC’s 31.0% for 2026 carries grade D and follows 3.1% a year earlier, so treat it as volatile. The sector also includes lessors and equipment rental. Canada’s all-industry average was 19.2% in 2026.

#### The software stack

**The ledger.** Yardi says it “holds the majority of the market share for residential and commercial real estate software solutions in Canada”, and has sold Breeze here since 2018. Buildium (part of RealPage), Entrata, MRI and Rent Manager are present. AppFolio reports its revenue comes “primarily from United States customers”. (Vendor-stated)

**Around the ledger.** Canadian tools handle listings (Rentsync, which owns Rentals.ca and RentFaster.ca), leasing (Rhenti), screening and rent guarantees (SingleKey), rent reporting (FrontLobby) and BC listings (liv.rent). Strata firms add BuildingLink, Condo Control, PowerStrata and StrataStation.

**Rent payment.** No source measures how Canadians pay rent. Among people who still write cheques regularly, rent is the most common reason. (Payments Canada)

**Large operators.** In a vendor-sponsored survey of 50 large Canadian multifamily operators, 44% said they had fully deployed AI, and only 11% relied on their property management software for it. (EliseAI, 2026) Among US property managers, 33% use AI or automation. (Buildium/NARPM 2027 report, US)

*03 · What to do next*

## Decide before you automate. *Measure before anyone promises savings.*

The firms that will gain most are not the ones that buy the most tools. They are the ones that know how their work is done, who decides what, and where their information lives. That is where we start with every firm.

#### Map the work

We sit with the people who run the day-to-day and write down how leasing, maintenance, accounting and owner work actually happen, and where the hours go.

#### Write down the decisions

For each workflow: what starts it, what “done right” looks like, and when a person must step in. The same documents train a new hire and guide any automation.

#### Automate, measured and governed

Start with one workflow, measure it before and after, and keep the human checkpoints the rules and your clients expect.

### Start with one workflow.

Pick the workflow that takes your team the most time, and we start there with you: how it runs today, the decisions in it, and where a person must stay in the loop.

*04 · The rules*

## Where a person must stay *in the loop.*

Property management is licensed work with a trust account at its centre. Some decisions are a person’s by law, and others should stay with a person by choice. Good AI design starts from that list.

| Rule | What it requires | Where a person stays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BC Real Estate Services Rules, s.80(c) and s.72(2) | Monthly trust reconciliation prepared within five weeks of month-end, then reviewed, dated and initialled by a managing broker or their designate. | The sign-off |
| BCFSA guidance on licensed activity | Collecting rent and deposits is rental property management, a licensed activity. | It cannot be handed to an unlicensed third party, software provider included |
| BC Residential Tenancy Act | Landlord-use, purchaser-use and caretaker notices are generated through the government’s web portal (since 18 July 2024); landlord-use notices give three months (since 18 June 2025). | The decision to end a tenancy |
| Alberta Residential Tenancies Act | No rent cap; one increase per 365 days; notices dated and signed by the landlord. | The signature |
| BC Strata Property Act | Depreciation reports every five years; the 2026 deadline has passed in the three largest districts, the rest are due by 1 July 2027; 10% minimum contingency contribution. | Council decisions and owner votes |
| Alberta Condominium Property Act | Amendments in force 15 February 2026; condominium dispute tribunal open since 1 April 2026, funded at $9 per unit per year. | Board resolutions, chargebacks |
| BCFSA AI Guideline (22 February 2024) | Warns that tenant-screening AI “may inadvertently rank individuals differently based on factors like race, gender, or language”, and that vendors could reuse strata data. | Screening decisions and vendor choice |
| Competition Bureau guidance on algorithmic pricing | Overriding a price recommendation should be no harder than accepting it. | Rent-setting |
| Federal AI law | The proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act died in January 2025. The privacy bill tabled in June 2026, still at first reading, adds transparency for automated decisions but no AI act. | Firms set their own guardrails for now |
| BC PST on property management (Budget 2026) | Announced for non-residential property management, then paused on 18 September 2026 with no end date. | A risk to watch for commercial managers |

*Appendix · The evidence · A1*

## A large market, run by small firms, *on the edge of a shift.*

The rest of this report is the evidence behind sections 01 to 04: the market, where the rent and the managers are, who manages, and how the work gets done.

British Columbia and Alberta renters pay about $26 billion a year in rent. Most of the businesses that manage those homes are small: well over half have fewer than five staff. The large operators are consolidating and centralising, and they have started to use AI to answer, triage and report at a scale no small office can staff. The same tools are now within reach of independent and mid-sized firms. What decides who benefits is not size, it is whether the work is written down well enough to hand to software.

- **Rent paid: $26.0B** (BC $15.9B · Alberta $10.1B). Renter households × average rent, per year. (Estimate)
- **Through a manager: $7.1B** (range $4.0B – $9.7B). Rent on homes run by a third-party manager. (Assumption range)
- **Management fee pool: $0.94B** (range $0.54B – $1.40B). Residential and strata management fees, per year. (Assumption range)
- **Industry revenue: $3.0B** (BC $1.92B · Alberta $1.08B (2024)). All property-manager revenue, including commercial and on-site services. (Statistics Canada)

### What the research says

#### Most rent never passes a professional manager

Of the $26B paid in rent, we estimate $4B to $10B goes through a third-party manager. The rest sits with owners who manage their own homes, from a basement suite to a 500-suite portfolio. That remainder is the growth market for every firm, large or small.

#### A market of micro-firms

BC has 976 property-management locations with employees and Alberta 672. 57% have one to four staff and only 76 have fifty or more. Another 6,001 operate with no employees at all. (Statistics Canada, June 2026)

#### Three districts hold 70% of the rent

Metro Vancouver ($10.4B), the Calgary region ($4.4B) and the Edmonton region ($3.6B) account for seven of every ten rent dollars. Greater Victoria and Central Okanagan follow. The map shows every district.

#### Alberta tenancies turn over twice as fast

In purpose-built rentals, 24.7% of Calgary units and 27.8% of Edmonton units changed tenants in the past year, against 11.6% in Metro Vancouver. Lethbridge reached 37.8%. Every move-out is a listing, a showing, an inspection, a deposit and a lease. (CMHC, Oct 2025)

#### Supply is growing faster than managers

Rental starts in Alberta’s larger centres rose from 3,026 in 2020 to 19,681 in 2025. BC’s strata sector has 807,738 strata lots, and its condo owners’ association said in 2023 the province “could use 300 more managers tomorrow”.

#### The big operators are pulling ahead

In Calgary and Edmonton, a handful of owner-operators run about one in five purpose-built units. The three largest BC strata managers that publish counts claim the equivalent of about one in seven strata lots. National and regional managers have bought at least nine BC and Alberta management businesses, divisions or contract books since 2023.

#### AI use in real estate is rising, but shallow

18.7% of Canadian real estate businesses used AI in 2026, slightly below the 19.2% average for all industries. Real estate was ahead of the economy in 2024 (8.1% against 6.1%) and has now fallen just behind. In the US, only 8% of property managers had fully automated any workflow. (Statistics Canada) (Buildium survey)

#### The rules keep a person in the loop

In BC, a managing broker or someone they designate signs off every monthly trust reconciliation. Collecting rent is licensed work. BC’s regulator has issued AI guidance warning about bias in tenant screening. No AI-specific law yet applies, so firms that set their own guardrails now will not have to retrofit them later.

*A4 · Who manages*

## Four kinds of operator. *One long tail.*

We group operators into four bands instead of naming them. Size comes from what each band manages in the districts it covers. Independents are by far the largest band by number of firms, and regional managers carry most of the managed units outside the national firms. This report is written for both: every firm below the national scale.

#### REITs, pension funds and public housing

Own and run their own buildings. They are not competitors for management contracts, but they set what tenants expect.

- About one in five purpose-built units in Calgary and Edmonton belongs to a handful of them. (Firm filings)
- Public and non-profit providers run more than 68,000 homes in BC (2022) and about 12,000 in Calgary and Edmonton.
- In-house apps, central call centres and new ERP systems.

#### Three or more provinces

Strata, rental and commercial managers with national back offices, proprietary portals and acquisition programmes.

- Earn much of their revenue from services around the building. At one listed national manager, management fees are 16% of revenue and on-site services 56%. (Annual report)
- At least nine acquisitions of BC and Alberta management businesses, divisions or contract books since 2023, by national and regional buyers.

#### 1,000+ units, one or two provinces

Mid-sized and large strata and rental managers, mostly in Metro Vancouver, Calgary and Edmonton. The largest of them are consolidating.

- 224 property-manager locations have 20 to 199 employees. (Statistics Canada)
- The three largest BC strata managers that publish counts claim about 120,000 managed units between them, mostly strata. That is the equivalent of about one in seven of BC’s 807,738 strata lots. (Firm-stated)
- In Alberta the three largest claim about 66,000 condo units, about one in five condo homes. (Firm-stated)

#### Under 1,000 units

Owner-led firms and brokerage divisions, often in one district. Their edge is local knowledge and personal service.

- 932 of the 1,648 employer locations have one to four staff, plus 6,001 with no employees. (Statistics Canada)
- Fees cluster at 10% of rent; leasing at half a month’s rent in BC.

*A5 · How the work gets done*

## A fee of about ten per cent, *and a calendar full of deadlines.*

Residential management is priced as a share of rent, with separate charges for finding a tenant. Strata management is priced per unit per month. The work behind the fee is mostly coordination: tenants, owners, trades, councils, regulators and the trust account, each with its own dates.

- **10%** Most common monthly management fee, from 18 published fee schedules (range 8% to 12.5%, plus GST). (Firm-stated)
- **½ month** Typical BC leasing fee. Alberta firms charge a flat $450 to $795. Renewals are often free, otherwise $100 to $250.
- **$35 · $25** Average strata or condo management fee per unit per month, BC and Alberta. One large Vancouver firm quotes $25 to $55 for full service. (Industry dataset)
- **26% · 23%** Industry operating margin, BC and Alberta, 2024 (preliminary). An industry total before tax, which counts sole owners’ own pay as profit. (Statistics Canada)

### Where the hours go

No Canadian survey measures how property managers spend their week, so we report what the volumes show:

**Tenancy changes.** At Alberta’s turnover rates, a manager with 150 apartment units handles roughly 37 to 42 move-outs and new leases a year. In Metro Vancouver it is closer to 17.

**Disputes.** Alberta’s tenancy dispute service received 15,239 filings in 2024/25, up from 10,973 four years earlier; about 73% were landlords seeking possession. BC’s Residential Tenancy Branch now hears 78.6% of disputes within its service standard, up from 5.2% in 2021/22. (Government)

**Strata deadlines.** Every BC strata corporation needs a depreciation report every five years. Those without one since 2020 had to have one by 1 July 2026 in Capital, Fraser Valley and Metro Vancouver, and must have one before 1 July 2027 elsewhere. (Government)

**Benchmarks.** In the US, property managers run about 49 units per staff member on average and 59 in the top quarter of firms. (NARPM, US, 2021)

## How the numbers were built.

Every figure on this page carries its source type: (Statistics Canada) or CMHC data, (Government) or regulator publications, (Firm-stated) claims by companies about themselves, (Survey) results from industry or vendor surveys, and (Estimate) or (Assumption range) for our own calculations. No property management firm is named. Software vendors are.

### District estimates

- **Renter households** per census division from the 2021 Census, grown by each division’s population growth to 2025 (Statistics Canada estimates).
- **Average rent** from CMHC’s October 2025 Rental Market Survey for divisions with a surveyed centre, weighted by units; elsewhere, the 2021 census average shelter cost for renters (which includes utilities) grown by CMHC’s 2020–2025 provincial rent growth. Rent paid = households × rent × 12.
- **Purpose-built rentals** from CMHC’s rental universe, minus known self-managed units of large owner-operators and public housing providers. Secondary rentals (condos, suites, houses) are renter households minus purpose-built and subsidised units.
- **Share run by a third-party manager**: no Canadian source publishes it, so we use a range. For Metro Vancouver, where several large owner-operators publish no counts, the low case uses a lower purpose-built share.
- **Fee pool** = managed rent × management fee, plus leasing fees at each district’s turnover rate, plus strata and condo units × the professionally managed share × the monthly per-unit fee × 12. Strata and condo units use census condominium dwellings in both provinces, so the provinces compare like with like. BC’s official count of 807,738 strata lots is higher because it includes bare-land, commercial and other lots.
- **PM businesses** per division are Statistics Canada’s counts for the wider industry group (which includes appraisers and other real-estate services), scaled by each province’s property-manager share.

| Assumption | Low | Base | High | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose-built units run by a third party (after known self-managers) | 30% | 45% | 60% | Large owners mostly self-manage; smaller building owners often hire a manager |
| Secondary rentals run by a third party | 15% | 25% | 35% | Most condo and suite owners self-manage. BC small investors hold 49.4% of rental property by assessed value |
| Strata and condo units professionally managed | 70% | 78% | 85% | In 2023 about 22,000 of BC’s 34,000 strata corporations were small and self-managed; small stratas hold few lots |
| Management fee, % of rent | 8% | 9% | 10% | Published fee schedules; 10% is the most common rate, with discounts on larger portfolios |
| Strata fee per unit per month, BC / Alberta | $30 / $20 | $35 / $25 | $45 / $35 | Industry dataset and published quotes |

Check: the base-case fee pool ($0.94B) is about a third of Statistics Canada’s $3.0B property-manager revenue for the two provinces. The rest is commercial management and services such as on-site staff, which this model leaves out.
